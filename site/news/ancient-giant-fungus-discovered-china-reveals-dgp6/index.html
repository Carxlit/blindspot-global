Ancient Giant Fungus Discovered in China Reveals Unexpected Genomic Structure

A five-pound fungal discovery in Yunnan Province traces back to the end of the dinosaurs. Its minimalist genetic toolkit challenges existing assumptions about plant decomposition and forest biology.

BS
Blindspot DeskNovember 15, 2026 · 2 min read · Primary source: Wired

Key Takeaways

  • The newly discovered fungus, Dianjunus rex, reaches up to 20 inches (50 centimeters) in height and weighs nearly five pounds (2.2 kilograms).
  • Molecular clock analysis conducted by the Kunming Institute of Botany dates the species divergence to approximately 65 million years ago.
  • Genomic sequencing reveals Dianjunus rex possesses only about half the decomposition-related genes found in its closest fungal relatives.
  • The species was identified in the Nanban tropical rainforest within China's Yunnan Province.
  • The organism constitutes an entirely new biological family within the phylum Ascomycota.

What Happened

A research team led by Associate Professor Indunil C. Senanayake at the Kunming Institute of Botany, a division of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, has formally documented a giant species of fungus named Dianjunus rex. Discovered in the Nanban tropical rainforest of Yunnan Province, the organism forms large whitish fruiting bodies that grow up to 20 inches tall and weigh nearly five pounds.

According to findings published by the team, the species belongs to the phylum Ascomycota—the largest fungal phylum containing over 64,000 known species. However, structural and genetic analysis confirmed that Dianjunus rex represents an entirely new biological family with no close living relatives identified to date.

The Context

Yunnan Province is globally recognized for its complex biodiversity, with over 6,000 documented fungal species and estimates suggesting up to 100,000 may exist in the region. The discovery of such a large macro-fungus underscores how much macro-organismal diversity remains unmapped in East Asian forest ecosystems.

Through DNA analysis, researchers reconstructed a molecular clock indicating that Dianjunus rex diverged from its nearest evolutionary ancestors approximately 65 million years ago. This timeline coincides with the Cretaceous-Paleogene extinction event, a period characterized by massive plant die-offs that provided vast ecological niches for decomposing organisms.

The Blind Spot

The primary scientific anomaly surrounding Dianjunus rex is not its physical scale, but its genetic composition. Standard genomic models dictate that macro-fungi capable of producing multi-pound fruiting bodies require an extensive array of plant-decomposing enzymes to harvest environmental carbon efficiently.

Genomic sequencing, however, revealed that Dianjunus rex possesses only roughly half the decomposition-related genes of its closest functional relatives. Researchers hypothesize that the fungus leads a latent dual existence: inhabiting living tree tissues as an endophyte without causing damage, then rapidly utilizing stored host nutrients once the tree dies to erect its tall spore-dispersing structures.

Winners and Losers

The primary beneficiaries of this research are bio-industrial sectors and research institutions focused on enzyme discovery and genomic mapping. Access to novel evolutionary pathways provides valuable blueprints for metabolic engineering and synthetic biology applications.

Conversely, standard ecological models for carbon cycling face disruption. If major biomass-building fungi bypass traditional decomposition pathways through latent endophytic relationships, existing climate and forest ecosystem calculations may require recalibration.

What to Watch

Future field studies will focus on isolating Dianjunus rex inside living host trees to confirm its endophytic phase and trace its exact nutrient accumulation mechanics.

Observers should also monitor whether the Kunming Institute of Botany identifies related species within the newly established biological family across Southeast Asian forest corridors.
