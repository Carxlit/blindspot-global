Inside Bukele's military intervention in Haiti and the hidden risks of outsourced security

El Salvador's confirmation of an airstrike against a major Haitian gang leader highlights a radical shift toward extraterritorial military intervention. Beneath the celebrations in Port-au-Prince lie urgent questions about civilian risks and long-term governance.

BS
Blindspot DeskNovember 19, 2026 · 2 min read · Primary source: Euronews

Key Takeaways

  • President Nayib Bukele confirmed on Wednesday that Salvadoran forces attacked and destroyed the luxury mansion of gang leader Jeff Larose.
  • El Salvador deployed about 2,500 troops to Haiti following an attack on four Salvadoran soldiers on 18 October.
  • The strike follows a previous attack on 8 November targeting the residence of Wilson Joseph, leader of the 400 Mawozo gang.
  • Jeff Larose controls the community of Canaan north of Port-au-Prince and leads one of the country's most structured criminal organizations.

What Happened

According to Euronews, Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele confirmed on Wednesday that his forces launched an airstrike that destroyed the luxurious mansion of Jeff Larose, a prominent leader of Haiti's criminal underworld. Bukele published video footage on the social network X showing Jeff posing in front of his property, followed by Salvadoran helicopters approaching the structure and opening fire with subsequent columns of smoke rising from the site.

The operation occurred on the anniversary of the Battle of Vertières, an 1803 conflict that secured Haiti's independence from France. For now, authorities have not confirmed whether Larose was killed in the bombing or if he was inside the building at the time of the strike.

The Context

The airstrike follows a similar operation on 8 November targeting the residence of Wilson Joseph, leader of the 400 Mawozo gang. Larose and Joseph have operated as key allies and primary criminal bosses in the capital. The military actions follow El Salvador's deployment of approximately 2,500 troops to Haiti last month, initiated after four Salvadoran soldiers were attacked and went missing on 18 October in gang-controlled territory.

In Port-au-Prince, the intervention has drawn vocal public support. Thousands of residents took to the streets for two consecutive days to celebrate the operation and chant Bukele's name, reflecting profound desperation amid prolonged gang violence.

The Blind Spot

While public demonstrations in Haiti celebrate foreign intervention as deliverance, international analysts note significant structural risks that mainstream coverage frequently overlooks. According to Haiti expert Diego Da Rin of the International Crisis Group, Canaan is one of the country's most structured and territorially entrenched organizations.

The integration of attack helicopters and explosive drones significantly increases operational capabilities against gangs, but it simultaneously amplifies the threat to civilian populations caught in densely populated areas. Furthermore, military experts warn that destroying infrastructure does not equate to dismantling criminal networks, leaving a critical vacuum in holding retaken ground against potential reprisals.

Winners and Losers

El Salvador gains considerable international visibility and projects hard power beyond its borders, cementing Bukele's standing as a formidable regional security actor. Conversely, traditional multilateral bodies and local Haitian governance structures are sidelined, underscoring the inability of domestic institutions to secure the state.

Ordinary residents of Port-au-Prince experience immediate relief from high-profile gang dominance, yet they continue to bear the physical and security costs of high-intensity kinetic operations conducted without transparent institutional oversight.

What to Watch

Key indicators moving forward include the ability of foreign deployed troops to maintain territorial control without provoking catastrophic retaliatory cycles from gangs against vulnerable civilians. Observers will also monitor whether El Salvador's intervention prompts formal multilateral adjustments or further unilateral deployments in the Caribbean nation.
